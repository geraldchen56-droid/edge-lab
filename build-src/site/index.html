Expected value

Used in: every game

Expected value is the long-run average of a random payoff. List every outcome, multiply each by its probability, and add.

EV = Σ pᵢ · xᵢ

Variance measures how far results spread around that average, and the standard deviation (σ) is its square root. EV tells you whether to trade; σ tells you how wide to quote and how much to bet.

Var = Σ pᵢ · (xᵢ − EV)² = E[X²] − EV²

Under the hood: every symbol in EV and variance

Reading EV = Σ pᵢ · xᵢ

XThe random quantity: a number you won't know until the event happens, such as the die roll.
iA label for each possible outcome, counting 1, 2, 3 … For a die, i runs from 1 to 6.
xᵢThe payoff of outcome i. For a die, x₁ = 1, x₂ = 2 … x₆ = 6.
pᵢThe probability of outcome i, between 0 and 1. All the pᵢ together add to exactly 1.
pᵢ · xᵢThat one outcome's contribution to the average.
ΣGreek capital sigma, meaning "sum over every i": do the thing for each outcome and add the results.
EV, E[X], μThree names for the same answer. μ (mu) is the usual symbol once you use it inside other formulas.

Why multiply by p? Picture rolling a die 600 times. You get about 100 of each face, so the total is about 100·1 + 100·2 + … + 100·6 = 2,100, and the average is 2,100 ÷ 600 = 3.5. Divide each count by 600 and the counts become probabilities: (1/6)·1 + (1/6)·2 + … + (1/6)·6. EV is an ordinary average where each outcome counts in proportion to how often it happens.

Reading Var(X) = Σ pᵢ · (xᵢ − μ)²

μThe EV from above: the centre of the outcomes.
xᵢ − μThe deviation: how far outcome i lands from the centre. Negative means below the centre.
( … )²The squared deviation. Squaring makes every distance positive, and it punishes big misses far more than small ones (a miss of 3 counts 9, a miss of 1 counts 1).
pᵢ · ( … )²Each squared miss, weighted by how often it happens.
ΣAdd them up. Variance is the average squared distance from the centre.
σ = √VarStandard deviation (lower-case sigma). Taking the square root undoes the squaring, so the answer is back in the payoff's own units (pips, dollars) and you can compare it with the EV.

Why square instead of just averaging the deviations? Because the plain average deviation is always exactly zero. The centre is defined as the balance point, so misses above and below cancel. The table below proves it: the p·(x − μ) column always sums to 0. You could use absolute distance |x − μ| instead, but squares have one property that makes everything later work: variances of independent quantities add. That is where the √n rule comes from.

Why the shortcut E[X²] − μ² works. E[X²] means "the average of the squared payoffs", Σ pᵢxᵢ². Expand (x − μ)² = x² − 2μx + μ², then average each piece: E[X²] − 2μ·E[X] + μ² = E[X²] − 2μ² + μ² = E[X²] − μ². For a die: 91/6 − 3.5² = 15.17 − 12.25 = 2.92.

Linearity and how spread grows

Used in: Dice, Trading Game, Card Game

The expected value of a sum equals the sum of the expected values. This holds even when the parts are dependent, which is why you never list 36 outcomes to price two dice.

E[X + Y] = E[X] + E[Y]

Variances add only when the parts are independent. So σ grows with the square root of the number of parts: ten dice have ten times the EV of one, but only about 3.2 times the spread. This is why sums look like a bell curve and why your quote width should grow slower than your fair value.

σ(sum of n independent) = σ₁ · √n

Reading E[X + Y] = E[X] + E[Y]

E[ · ]"The expected value of" whatever is inside the brackets.
X, YTwo random quantities, such as die 1 and die 2.

Why it always holds. An average is "add everything, then divide", and the order you add things in doesn't matter. Over 600 rolls of two dice, the grand total of all the sums equals the total of die 1's column plus the total of die 2's column. Dependence changes which values appear together on the same row, but not what each column adds up to.

Cov(X, Y)Covariance: do X and Y tend to be high together (positive), move in opposite directions (negative), or ignore each other (zero)? It is the average of (X − μₓ)(Y − μᵧ).

Where √n comes from

Add n independent copies, each with variance σ₁². The variances add to n·σ₁², so σ = √(n·σ₁²) = σ₁·√n. Ten dice: 1.71 × √10 ≈ 5.40, against an EV of 35. In finance, this is why diversification works: holding many assets that aren't perfectly correlated grows expected return faster than it grows risk.

Shorthand
  • Sum → add EVs. n dice = 3.5n. n cards without replacement = 7n.
  • Spread → √n. n dice σ ≈ 1.71√n. Roughly 95% of outcomes fall within 2σ.
  • Known part → plug it in. One die shows 5: fair for two dice is 5 + 3.5 = 8.5.
  • Without replacement: unknown cards average the remaining deck, not 7.

Stop or continue

Used in: interview EV puzzles, Higher or Lower

When you get to choose during a game, solve from the last decision backwards. At each step, keep what you have if it beats the EV of continuing.

V₀ = (s+1)/2 Vₖ = E[ max(X, Vₖ₋₁) ]

Vₖ is the game's value when you have k re-rolls left on an s-sided die. The threshold rule is: keep any roll strictly above Vₖ₋₁.

Desk · re-roll game solver
1
Under the hood: reading Vₖ = E[ max(X, Vₖ₋₁) ]
sNumber of sides on the die.
VₖWhat the game is worth when you still have k re-rolls left and you play perfectly.
V₀ = (s+1)/2With no re-rolls left you must keep the next roll, so the game is worth the plain EV of one roll.
XThe roll you are about to see.
Vₖ₋₁What you'd be worth if you threw this roll away: the same game with one fewer re-roll.
max(X, Vₖ₋₁)Your payoff after seeing X: keep X, or swap it for the rest of the game. You take whichever is bigger.
E[ … ]Average that payoff over every roll you might see.

Worked: six-sided die, one re-roll

V₀ = 3.5. On a 1, 2 or 3, re-rolling (worth 3.5) beats keeping, so max = 3.5. On a 4, 5 or 6, keep it. So V₁ = (3.5 + 3.5 + 3.5 + 4 + 5 + 6) ÷ 6 = 25.5 ÷ 6 = 4.25.

With two re-rolls the bar rises to 4.25, so only 5 and 6 are kept: V₂ = (4 × 4.25 + 5 + 6) ÷ 6 = 28 ÷ 6 = 4.67.

Why work backwards? You can't value the first decision without knowing what continuing is worth. Only the last decision has a known continuation (the plain EV), so you start there and move back one step at a time. This method is called backward induction, and it is exactly how American options are priced on a binomial tree: at each node, take the max of exercising now or holding.

Shorthand
  • Six-sided die, one re-roll = 4.25. Two re-rolls = 4.67. Memorise these two.
  • Keep if roll > value of continuing. Never compare to the overall average.

Bayes in odds form

Used in: Event Contracts, Fermi, Trading Game, poker reads

Traders update probabilities in odds, because the arithmetic becomes one multiplication. Odds are p / (1 − p). A likelihood ratio (LR) says how many times more likely the news is if the event is true than if it is false.

posterior odds = prior odds × LR p = odds / (1 + odds)

Under the hood: prior, likelihood, posterior
HThe hypothesis: the thing you're unsure about, e.g. "it rains today".
EThe evidence: the news you observe, e.g. "the morning is overcast".
P(H)The prior: your probability before seeing the evidence (Latin prior, "earlier"). Usually a base rate, such as "it rains on 20% of days this month".
P(E | H)A likelihood: how often you'd see this evidence if H were true. The bar "|" is read "given". Example: on rainy days, mornings are overcast 70% of the time.
P(E | not H)How often you'd see the same evidence if H were false. Example: on dry days, 20% of mornings are overcast.
P(H | E)The posterior: your probability after the evidence (Latin posterior, "coming after"). This is what you actually want. Today's posterior becomes tomorrow's prior when new news arrives.
odds = p ÷ (1 − p)Chance for against chance against. 20% → 0.2 ÷ 0.8 = 0.25, said "1 to 4".
LRLikelihood ratio = P(E | H) ÷ P(E | not H). How many times more common the evidence is when H is true: 0.7 ÷ 0.2 = 3.5. This is the strength of the news.

Where the odds form comes from

Bayes' rule is P(H | E) = P(E | H) · P(H) ÷ P(E). Write the same rule for "not H", then divide one by the other. The awkward P(E) term cancels and you get posterior odds = prior odds × LR. Rain example: 0.25 × 3.5 = 0.875, and 0.875 ÷ 1.875 = 46.7%.

See it as 1,000 cases

The clearest way to understand a posterior is to count. Each dot below is one day. Once you see the evidence, you are standing on one of the solid dots, so throw away the faded ones. The posterior is the share of solid dots that are green.

The classic trap

P(E | H) is not P(H | E). Suppose a system catches 90% of fraudulent trades and wrongly flags 5% of good ones, and 1% of trades are fraud. A flagged trade feels 90% likely to be fraud, but: prior odds 1:99, LR = 0.90 ÷ 0.05 = 18, posterior odds = 18/99 ≈ 0.18, so p ≈ 15%. A low base rate swamps even strong evidence. Set the sliders to 1%, 90%, 5% to see it.

Shorthand
  • Convert, multiply, convert back. 20% → 1:4 → ×3 → 3:4 → 3/7 ≈ 43%.
  • LR = 1 means the news says nothing. LR below 1 moves you down.
  • Two independent clues → multiply both LRs.

Bet sizing with the Kelly criterion

Used in: Higher or Lower, Betting Board, poker bankroll, position sizing

Positive EV is not enough: bet too much and a losing run wipes you out. Kelly picks the fraction of bankroll that maximises long-run growth. With win probability p and net odds b (win b per 1 staked):

f* = (b·p − (1 − p)) / b = edge / odds even money: f* = 2p − 1

Betting twice Kelly gives roughly zero long-run growth, and more than that shrinks the bankroll even though every bet is +EV. Most professionals bet half Kelly, because their p is only an estimate.

Desk · 300 bets, three sizing rules, same luck
55%
Under the hood: reading f* = (b·p − q) ÷ b
f*The fraction of your current bankroll to stake. The star marks it as the optimal value.
pProbability you win the bet.
q = 1 − pProbability you lose.
bNet odds: profit per 1 staked if you win. Even money is b = 1. From decimal odds d, b = d − 1.
b·p − qEV per 1 staked: you win b with probability p and lose 1 with probability q. This is your edge.
÷ bScale down for long-shot payoffs, which swing more. So the result is edge divided by odds.

Why not just maximise EV?

Maximising EV says stake everything on every +EV bet, and the first loss takes you to zero. Wealth compounds by multiplying, not adding: +50% followed by −50% leaves you at 1.5 × 0.5 = 0.75 of where you started. So Kelly maximises the average growth rate, which is the expected logarithm of the multiplier on each bet:

g(f) = p · ln(1 + f·b) + q · ln(1 − f)

Here ln(1 + f·b) is the log of your wealth multiplier on a win, and ln(1 − f) is the log of the multiplier on a loss. Set the slope of g to zero, p·b ÷ (1 + f·b) = q ÷ (1 − f), and solving for f gives f* = (b·p − q) ÷ b.

Example: p = 0.55, b = 1 → f* = 0.10. Growth per bet = 0.55 · ln 1.1 + 0.45 · ln 0.9 = 0.0524 − 0.0474 = 0.0050, about 0.5% per bet, compounding.

Shorthand
  • Even money: bet your edge. 55% win → 10% of bankroll.
  • Negative EV → bet 0. Kelly never says bet on a losing proposition.
  • Half Kelly keeps about 75% of the growth with far smaller drawdowns.

1 · Card Game (market taking)

Skill: EV, edge detectionFirm echo: SIG

Someone quotes you a two-way price on a random quantity. You may buy at their ask, sell at their bid, or pass. Your only job is to compute fair value fast and compare.

  • Fair value above the ask → buy. Edge = fair − ask.
  • Fair value below the bid → sell. Edge = bid − fair.
  • Fair value inside the quote → pass. Trading there loses money on average.

2 · Market Making Dice Game

Skill: two-way pricing, width, inventory, event riskFirm echo: Optiver, Flow Traders

Now you are the one quoting. The contract settles at the sum of three dice. Five traders arrive one at a time and trade 1 lot against your quote if they like it. Most are noise traders. Some have seen one die. Midway, one die is revealed to everyone.

  • Centre your quote on fair value (10.5 to start).
  • Width pays for risk. Too tight and informed traders pick you off; too wide and nobody trades.
  • Skew after a fill. If you sold, you are short: raise both prices so the next fill is more likely to be a sale to you.
  • Re-centre instantly when the die is revealed. Fair = revealed die + 7.

4 · ETF Arbitrage

Skill: cross-asset pricing, speedFirm echo: IMC, Jane Street

An ETF is a basket. Its fair value, the net asset value (NAV), is what the shares inside it are worth. If the ETF trades away from NAV by more than your costs, you trade one against the other and lock in the gap.

  • NAV = Σ (shares per unit × stock price).
  • ETF ask below NAV − cost → buy ETF, sell the stocks. Profit = NAV − ask − cost.
  • ETF bid above NAV + cost → sell ETF, buy the stocks. Profit = bid − NAV − cost.
  • Otherwise no trade.

5 · Fermi Estimation

Skill: decomposition, calibrated intervalsFirm echo: most prop firms

Give a range you are 90% sure contains the true answer. You score for being right and for being narrow. Over many questions, about 9 in 10 should land inside. If 5 in 10 land, you are overconfident, which in trading means quoting too tight.

  • Break the quantity into factors you can guess (people × rate × time).
  • Estimate in powers of ten; multiply exponents.
  • Set the range by your uncertainty in each factor, then check: would you bet 9-to-1 on it?

Part III

Poker

Read the table, not the face.

Poker trains the same muscles as the games above: EV under uncertainty, sizing to edge, and reading whether the other side knows more than you. It also adds what the market games lack, a thinking opponent who adapts to you. Click the felt to run out the board.

Rules of No-Limit Texas Hold'em and hand ranks

  • Blinds: the two players left of the dealer button post a small and big blind (e.g. 1 and 2). The button rotates each hand.
  • Preflop: everyone gets two private cards. Action starts left of the big blind: fold, call, or raise.
  • Flop, turn, river: three, then one, then one shared card. A betting round follows each.
  • Showdown: the best five-card hand from your two cards plus the five shared cards wins the pot.
  • No-limit: you may bet any amount up to your whole stack at any time.

Pot odds, outs and the rule of 2 and 4

Facing a bet, calling is +EV when your chance of winning (equity) is higher than the share of the final pot you must put in.

required equity = call ÷ (pot after the call) = B ÷ (P + 2B)

Outs are the unseen cards that turn your hand into the winner. With one card to come, equity ≈ outs × 2%. With two cards to come (you're all-in on the flop), equity ≈ outs × 4%. Exact one-card equity on the turn is outs ÷ 46.

  • Rule of 4 only applies if you see both cards, i.e. when all-in.

Bluffing maths, minimum defence and position

A pure bluff of size B into pot P risks B to win P. It breaks even if the opponent folds often enough. Turn it around and you get the defender's rule: fold more than that and any two cards can bluff you profitably.

bluff needs folds ≥ B ÷ (P + B) minimum defence frequency = P ÷ (P + B)
Desk · bet sizing calculator
75
  • Position: acting last is a structural edge, because you see what others do first. Play more hands on the button, fewer under the gun.
  • Ranges, not hands: put the opponent on every hand they would play this way, weighted by likelihood. This is Bayes again: each action is evidence with a likelihood ratio.
  • Balance: bet your strong hands and some bluffs with the same size, in a ratio that makes the opponent indifferent. A pot-size river bet wants about 1 bluff for every 2 value bets.
  • Exploit: once you know a player folds too much, bluff them more. Game theory optimal (GTO) play is the unexploitable baseline; profit comes from deviating against mistakes.
  • Variance and tilt: a winning player can lose for weeks. Track results in big blinds per 100 hands (bb/100) over tens of thousands of hands, and never chase losses.
Shorthand
  • Pot-size bet: bluff needs 50% folds; defend 50%; value:bluff = 2:1.
  • Half-pot bet: bluff needs 33% folds; defend 67%; value:bluff = 3:1.
  • Same maths as quoting: an informed counterparty is a player who only bets the nuts.

The same ideas, renamed

Nothing here is a detour. Each tool from the games reappears under a new name in financial economics and econometrics.

The most important bridge is statistical significance. Is your strategy, or your poker win rate, real edge or luck? The answer is the t-statistic, the core test statistic of econometrics.

t = (mean edge per trade) × √n ÷ σ P(profitable over n) ≈ Φ(t)
Desk · edge or luck?
1,000
Under the hood: reading t = m · √n ÷ σ
mYour average edge per trade or hand: the EV of one repetition.
σThe standard deviation of one trade's result: how noisy each repetition is.
nHow many trades or hands you've played.
m · nExpected total profit. It grows in proportion to n.
σ · √nSpread of the total profit, from the √n rule. It grows more slowly.
tSignal ÷ noise = (m·n) ÷ (σ·√n) = m·√n ÷ σ. It counts how many standard deviations your result sits away from zero.
Φ(t)The normal distribution's cumulative probability: the chance that a bell-curve variable lands below t. Here, the chance of being ahead after n trades.
1.96The t at which only 5% of pure-luck results would look this good. Beyond it, econometricians call the edge "statistically significant".
